\title{Demand, Regionality, Entry Selectivity, and the Research Talent Pipeline in Portuguese Higher Education}
\author{Diogo}
\date{31 August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
This study evaluates linked empirical propositions about Portuguese higher education: whether placements in science and engineering are declining, how strongly demand and geography structure entry selectivity, whether external university rankings add predictive information beyond observed demand, and how these patterns may matter for the future research-talent pipeline. Comparable broad-area first-phase tables are observed for 2017, 2018 and 2020--2026; no 2019 field value is reconstructed. Registered STEM placements rise from 13,926 in 2017 to 15,181 in 2026 (+9.0\%), while their share of all first-phase placements falls by 0.64 percentage points. Normalising by the source-locked resident population aged exactly 18 gives an endpoint increase of +9.1\%, whereas the older 15--24 divided-by-ten cohort proxy gives only +1.3\%. The divergence shows that the broad proxy measures young-adult population scale rather than the registered entry-age cohort. Study B uses five exact DGES programmes across 2018--2020. Across 15 programme-year cells, log applicants per vacancy has median $R^2=0.598$ for the general-contingent cut-off and $R^2=0.552$ for the mean application grade of placed candidates; all 30 coefficients are positive, but no cell reaches the preregistered $R^2=0.80$ benchmark. DGES mobility matrices for 2023--2025 show same-district first-choice shares near 64--65\%, compared with 56--58\% for realised placements. In leave-one-parent-institution-out ranking models, Times Higher Education rankings improve both grade outcomes before conditioning on demand, but the incremental gain essentially disappears once contemporaneous demand is included. QS and ARWU provide substantially narrower support and no stable incremental grade-prediction gain. The ranking attenuation is predictive, not causal mediation evidence. The remaining research-pipeline analysis is therefore treated as a distinct downstream question rather than inferred from admissions alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Portuguese public discussion about higher education often combines three levels of analysis: immediate admission outcomes, the geography and selectivity of student choice, and the long-run supply of researchers. These levels are related, but they are not interchangeable. This paper constructs a unified empirical framework in which each is measured separately before their connections are examined.

The analysis is proposition-neutral. Public claims are translated into observable estimands and can receive supportive, contradictory, mixed, or insufficient evidence. Descriptive, predictive, associational, and causal language are kept separate. The present evidence covers medium-run STEM placements, a registered five-programme demand/selectivity panel, recent regionality, and provider-specific ranking prediction. The R\&D pipeline remains a separate downstream evidence task.

\section{Empirical propositions}
\subsection{Science and engineering placements}
The first proposition is that placements in science and engineering have entered a sustained decline. A meaningful evaluation must separate realised placements from applicant demand, first preferences, places offered, occupancy, field share and population scale. A decline on one margin need not imply decline on the others.

\subsection{Regionality, demand pressure, and entry grades}
The second proposition has three components: that higher-education choice is strongly regional; that applicants per place account for most differences in entry grades; and that ranking information has little incremental predictive value once demand is observed. These questions are analysed separately to avoid conflating geography, demand, selectivity and reputation.

\subsection{The R\&D talent pipeline}
The third proposition is that high-performing research units may face future constraints when the educational pipeline feeding their fields and host institutions weakens. Because future capacity constraints are not always directly observable, the design distinguishes current pipeline exposure from realised downstream research outcomes.

\section{Data}
The full study uses official Concurso Nacional de Acesso (CNA) statistics, DGES comparative course statistics and mobility matrices, DGEEC RAIDES and science-and-technology statistics, FCT R\&D-unit evaluations, official demographic data, and historical ranking information from QS, Times Higher Education and ARWU.

The CNA archive is treated as a sequence of historical source formats rather than as one immutable schema. Historical programme identities, capacity, application, placement and grade measures are reconciled explicitly. Historical mobility geography is preserved rather than silently coerced into modern district labels. Ranking providers are analysed separately, publication timing is constrained to information available before the relevant application deadline, official rank bands remain categorical, and provider values are not redistributed.

\subsection{Broad-area first-phase tables}
The medium-run analysis uses complete official broad-area first-phase tables for 2017, 2018 and 2020--2026. The 2017 official document labels the relevant table \emph{Quadro XII}; later observed vintages use \emph{Quadro V}. Each observed year contains 23 broad study areas with vacancies, first-choice candidates, placements and remaining vacancies.

The official 2019 first-phase statistical index lists a placement summary, establishment and course comparisons, mobility, cut-offs and programme-pair statistics, but it does not list a comparable complete broad-area placement table.\footnote{Direcao-Geral do Ensino Superior, \href{https://www.dges.gov.pt/guias/pdfs/statcol/2019/}{2019 CNA statistical archive}.} National 2019 totals therefore cannot identify field composition, and no broad-area observation is interpolated or inferred from neighbouring years.

For every observed year, the broad-area data satisfy
\[
\sum_g V_{gt}=V_t,\qquad
\sum_g A^{(1)}_{gt}=A_t,\qquad
\sum_g P_{gt}=P_t,
\]
before STEM aggregation. The nine observed years therefore contribute $9\times23=207$ area-year observations.

\subsection{Demographic series}
The preferred demographic denominator is the resident population aged exactly 18 from INE indicator \texttt{0001223}, \emph{Populacao residente (Serie longa, inicio 1970 - N.o) por Sexo e Idade; Anual}. The released slice is Portugal (\texttt{PT}), total sex (\texttt{T}/HM), age code \texttt{224} (18 years), for 2016--2025. Competition year $t$ is matched to population year $t-1$, so those ten population values cover the observed 2017--2026 admission endpoints. The age-18 population is 110,479 in 2016 and 110,376 in 2025, almost unchanged between the endpoint references. INE metadata documents a change from census-based estimates through 2020 to administrative-base estimates from 2021 onward; the 2020--2021 transition is therefore treated as a methodological comparability break rather than automatically interpreted as demographic change.

The older demographic sensitivity uses the INE resident population aged 15--24 disseminated by PORDATA.\footnote{PORDATA, \href{https://www.pordata.pt/en/portugal/resident+population+of+working+age+total+and+by+age+group-3245}{resident population by age group}; source entity INE, last updated 22 June 2026.} That broad population rises from 1,101,839 in 2016 to 1,185,234 in 2025, a 7.6\% increase. It is retained as a secondary sensitivity and is never relabelled as population aged 18.

\subsection{Study B programme panel and mobility data}
Study B fixes five exact DGES programme identities: 9081 Economia, 9119 Engenharia Informática, 9147 Gestão, 9219 Psicologia and 9500 Enfermagem. Official \emph{StatsCurso19} and \emph{StatsCurso20} comparative tables provide 2018--2020 first-phase counts and grades. The 2019 overlap is reconciled exactly before deduplication. The source layer contains 87 stable programme--establishment units and 261 canonical programme--establishment-year observations.

Regionality uses the registered DGES 2023--2025 origin--destination matrices for first choices and realised placements. Primary same-area statistics are restricted to origins explicitly comparable with destination districts; autonomous regions remain in the matrices and coverage denominators rather than being relabelled.

\subsection{Historical ranking data}
QS, Times Higher Education and ARWU are analysed independently. The eligible edition must have been published before the relevant first-phase application deadline. Exact published ranks remain numeric; published rank bands remain categorical and are never converted to midpoints. Candidate-only or otherwise unranked institutions remain unranked. The private 64-row historical ranking panel is hash-bound for reproducibility but is not committed or redistributed.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Access and placement}
For field group $g$ and competition year $t$, define placement share
\[
S_{gt}=\frac{P_{gt}}{P_{\cdot t}},
\]
occupancy
\[
O_{gt}=\frac{P_{gt}}{V_{gt}},
\]
and first-choice pressure
\[
F_{gt}=\frac{A^{(1)}_{gt}}{V_{gt}}.
\]
The broad-area table reports first-choice candidates, not total applications, so $F_{gt}$ is not relabelled as applicants per vacancy. The programme-level analysis separately uses total applicants per vacancy.

The STEM grouping contains three broad ISCED-F-compatible components: 05, natural sciences, mathematics and statistics; 06, information and communication technologies; and 07, engineering, manufacturing and construction. The seven published source areas beneath those components are retained before aggregation.

\subsection{Medium-run descriptive paths}
For each component and the all-STEM aggregate, the fitted count description is
\[
\log P_{gt}=\alpha_g+\beta_g t+e_{gt}.
\]
The annual percentage path is reported as $100[\exp(\widehat\beta_g)-1]$. Placement share, occupancy and first-choice pressure are summarised by linear percentage-point gradients in calendar year. Calendar year itself is the regressor, so the missing 2019 observation remains a two-year interval between 2018 and 2020. The movement from 2018 to 2020 is therefore not labelled a year-on-year change.

A pre-specified sensitivity excludes 2020 and 2021. These observations are administrative population counts rather than a probability sample, and nine observed years are too few for credible data-driven break selection. The fitted slopes and $R^2$ values are descriptive summaries; no sampling p-values or causal interpretation are attached to them.

\subsection{Exact age-18 demographic normalisation}
Let $N_{18,t-1}$ denote the resident population aged exactly 18 in the calendar year preceding competition year $t$. The preferred demographic rate is
\[
H^{18}_{gt}=1000\frac{P_{gt}}{N_{18,t-1}}.
\]
The denominator is source-locked from INE indicator \texttt{0001223}. The 2020--2021 estimation-method change is carried explicitly as a comparability caveat. Raw counts and national placement shares remain separate outcomes; this demographic rate is descriptive rather than causal.

\subsection{Broad-cohort demographic sensitivity}
Let $N_{15:24,t-1}$ denote the resident population aged 15--24 in the calendar year preceding competition year $t$. The historical sensitivity defines an average single-year cohort proxy
\[
C^{\mathrm{proxy}}_t=\frac{N_{15:24,t-1}}{10},
\]
and the secondary normalised placement rate
\[
H^{\mathrm{proxy}}_{gt}=1000\frac{P_{gt}}{C^{\mathrm{proxy}}_t}.
\]
This denominator is deliberately weaker than the exact age-18 count. It tests whether endpoint changes in placements are commensurate with broad young-adult population scale, not entry-age cohort size.

\subsection{Demand, regionality and ranking}
For programme $p$, institution $i$ and year $t$, define demand pressure
\[
D_{ipt}=\frac{A_{ipt}}{V_{ipt}}.
\]
Within programme-year cells, the registered descriptive association is
\[
y_{ipt}=\alpha_{pt}+\beta_{pt}\log D_{ipt}+\varepsilon_{ipt}.
\]
The primary summaries are the 15 programme-year $R^2$ values for each grade outcome, together with the sign of the 30 log-demand coefficients.

For comparable district origins, the same-district flow share is
\[
R_t=\frac{\sum_{d\in\mathcal C_t}F_{ddt}}{\sum_{o\in\mathcal C_t}\sum_d F_{odt}}.
\]
Conditional destination entropy and normalised mutual information supplement the diagonal share.

Ranking prediction is evaluated by leave-one-parent-institution-out validation. The model ladder is
\[
M_0:\ \text{programme}+\text{year},
\]
\[
M_R:\ M_0+\text{ranking},
\]
\[
M_D:\ M_0+\log(D),
\]
\[
M_{DR}:\ M_D+\text{ranking}.
\]
Baseline and ranking models are trained on identical provider-ranked rows and scored on identical held-out observations. A held-out row is scored only when both its programme/year structure and ranking representation are supported in training. Unsupported rows are reported explicitly rather than mapped to a reference category. RMSE and MAE are predictive metrics, not causal-effect estimates.

\subsection{R\&D pipeline exposure}
R\&D units are linked to host institutions and feeder fields through explicit weights. Pipeline stages are measured separately from first-time entry through doctoral output and research personnel. Composite risk indices, if used, are exploratory and accompanied by component-level results.

\subsection{The 2025 trough}
The recent window identifies a sharp short-run event. Registered STEM placements fall from 15,365 in 2024 to 13,183 in 2025 (-14.2\%) and then recover to 15,181 in 2026 (+15.2\%). National first-phase placements show the same broad discontinuity, falling from 49,963 to 43,899 before recovering to 49,991. The medium-run evidence therefore treats 2025 as a pronounced trough rather than extrapolating it into a monotonic historical path.

\section{Reliability conditions}
The empirical results are reported only after the following checks:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item each observed broad-area year contains the same 23 source areas and reconciles exactly to published national controls;
\item the missing 2019 field composition remains missing, and non-consecutive observations are not labelled year-on-year changes;
\item the 2020--2021 exclusion sensitivity is evaluated separately;
\item the exact age-18 denominator is source-locked to INE indicator \texttt{0001223}, Portugal, total sex and age code \texttt{224}, with competition year $t$ mapped to population year $t-1$;
\item the 2020--2021 INE population-estimation methodology break is recorded explicitly;
\item the 15--24 divided-by-ten denominator remains labelled as a broad-cohort proxy rather than an exact age-18 count;
\item all duplicated 2019 programme observations reconcile across DGES comparative-table vintages before canonicalisation;
\item stable programme selection precedes grade-model attrition and missing grades are not imputed;
\item regionality uses only explicitly comparable district origins for the primary same-district statistic;
\item ranking editions obey the frozen pre-deadline timing rule, rank bands remain categorical, and provider values are not redistributed;
\item LOPO ranking comparisons use identical paired samples and exclude held-out rows without structural or ranking support.
\end{itemize}
These checks constrain interpretation but do not create a causal design.

\section{Discussion}
The medium-run evidence does not support a simple statement that absolute first-phase STEM placements have continuously declined since 2017. The 2026 all-STEM count is higher than both the 2017 and 2023 counts, and the 2025 contraction is followed by a large rebound. Yet that observation is only one margin. The all-STEM share of national placements is slightly lower than in 2017 and first-choice pressure has weakened. The preferred exact-age-normalised endpoint, however, remains close to the raw count result: +9.1\% versus +9.0\%.

The broad-cohort sensitivity tells a different story, with only +1.3\% endpoint growth. That difference is informative rather than contradictory. The exact denominator measures the registered entry-age cohort, while the 15--24/10 proxy measures a broad young-adult population scale that grew substantially over the period. The proxy is therefore retained as a robustness comparison but does not replace the exact-age result.

The component results are more informative than the aggregate. Natural sciences, mathematics and statistics are lower in raw, exact-age and broad-proxy terms. ICT expands strongly from its 2017 base. Engineering, manufacturing and construction rises in raw and exact-age terms but only modestly under the broad proxy. Consequently, ``fewer placements'', ``lower relative share'', ``weaker first-choice pressure'', ``lower placements relative to age-18 population'' and ``lower placements relative to broad young-adult population scale'' are distinct empirical statements.

Study B adds three further distinctions. First, applicants per vacancy are consistently positively associated with entry grades when programme identity is held fixed, but explanatory strength is heterogeneous and never reaches the preregistered threshold for an almost-complete explanation. Second, expressed first choices are strongly regional, yet realised placements are systematically less same-district concentrated, showing that preference geography and allocation geography are not the same object. Third, the broad THE ranking sample contains incremental grade-prediction information before demand is included, but essentially no residual out-of-institution grade-prediction gain after contemporaneous demand enters the model. That attenuation is consistent with demand already containing much of the predictive information associated with ranking or reputation, but the design does not identify a causal mediation pathway.

Provider asymmetry matters. THE retains nearly complete out-of-institution support, whereas QS and ARWU lose large fractions of eligible rows under the frozen ranking and structural-support rules. Their weaker and less stable results should therefore not be interpreted as symmetric evidence about the substantive quality of the providers themselves. They are evidence about predictive performance under the registered historical coverage available for this panel.

The remaining limitations are substantive. The broad-area field composition is not observed for 2019, the medium-run series begins only in 2017, and broad-area tables do not contain total programme applications. The exact age-18 series is source-locked for the current 2016--2025 denominator window, but interpretation across 2020--2021 must respect INE's estimation-method change. Study B covers five programmes and only three admission years for the ranking analysis; the regionality window is 2023--2025 rather than the same historical period. Rankings are imperfect proxies for reputation and their publication structures differ across providers. Finally, no current result establishes that admissions patterns have already constrained R\&D-unit performance. That downstream pipeline question requires explicit linkage to feeder fields, postgraduate progression and research personnel.

\section{Conclusion}
Between 2017 and 2026, raw registered STEM placements rise by 9.0\%, their share of all first-phase placements falls by 0.64 percentage points, and the preferred exact age-18-normalised endpoint rate rises by 9.1\%. The older broad 15--24/10 sensitivity rises by only 1.3\%, showing that demographic conclusions depend on whether the denominator represents the entry-age cohort or a much broader young-adult population scale. Component paths diverge substantially, so the medium-run evidence cannot be represented by one claim of continuous aggregate decline.

Across five matched programmes in 2018--2020, applicants per vacancy are an important but heterogeneous predictor of entry grades: all 30 registered log-demand coefficients are positive, while no programme-year model reaches the frozen $R^2=0.80$ benchmark. Recent mobility matrices show that roughly two thirds of comparable first choices remain within the applicant's district, but realised placements are less geographically concentrated. In the ranking analysis, THE rankings improve out-of-institution grade prediction before demand is included, whereas the incremental gain essentially disappears after contemporaneous demand enters; QS and ARWU do not show stable incremental value under their narrower supported samples.

Taken together, the Study B evidence supports a structured interpretation rather than a single-factor story: demand, geography and institutional reputation are related dimensions of selectivity, but none should be substituted mechanically for another. The remaining unresolved empirical question is downstream: whether and where these admission-pipeline patterns translate into constraints on Portuguese research capacity.
